# Role review: caregiver trainee "Maya" (simulated, 2026-11-15)

> **Simulated perspective** produced by a subagent. It is not a real user.
>
> Maya is a hearing trainee home caregiver with two Deaf clients: an elderly ASL signer with arthritis, and a 4-year-old Deaf girl. She has about 10 minutes a day.

## 1. Needs, ranked
1. **Safety:** STOP, WAIT, HOT, NO, HELP, and how to get attention (tap, wave, flicking the light).
2. **Care basics:** PAIN (where and how bad), MEDICINE, TOILET, EAT, DRINK, TIRED, COLD, FINISHED, MORE, WASH HANDS.
3. **Repair and politeness:** AGAIN, SLOWER, SHOW ME, I'M LEARNING, SORRY, THANK YOU. Isolation is a big risk for older Deaf people, so greetings matter.
4. **Numbers:** a 1–10 pain scale, pill counts, times.
5. **Fingerspelling:** medication names, family and doctor names, my own name.
6. **Reading his fingerspelling:** arthritic, small, low.
7. **Facial expressions** for asking questions.

## 2. Would it help?
Partly. Fingerspelling is her safety net for medication and street names.
- The first-person ghost hand helps her, because she flips video demonstrations the wrong way.
- "Can't see well enough to grade" makes her trust it *more*.

But fingerspelling is fifth on her list. Room objects aren't her job, and the tutor hand's perfect fingers don't look like her client's hands.

## 3. What's missing
- A **"my clients" word list**: names, medications, the dog's name.
- **Caregiving signs**, even 20, with the facial expression. Otherwise, a handoff to a trusted course.
- **Numbers** and a pain scale.
- A **reading mode with realistic hands**: smaller, lower, softer shapes, letters running together.
- **"Reviewed by a Deaf signer"**, shown visibly.
- A left-hand option, and spelling as a flowing movement rather than frozen poses.

## 4. Would she come back tomorrow?
For a week, to get the alphabet. Streaks don't move her. What would:
- it opens in under 30 seconds;
- today's 5 minutes use her clients' words;
- it tells her "you can now spell Mr. H's name".

## 5a. Multiplayer with hints
- **With another trainee or the girl's mum:** yes. It's real practice and less embarrassing.
- **With the child:** no. She's 4, the Quest is 10+, and there's one headset.
- **With the client:** only if he wants to, and the app must **never grade him**. He is the teacher.

## 5b. Translation helper
Tempting, and that's the danger.
- If it read "two pills" as "ten", she would act on it.
- A headset over her eyes is rude when faces carry meaning.
- It probably can't read arthritic or child hands.

Use it only on recorded signing for practice, **never with a client for anything medical**. That needs an interpreter, writing, and teach-back.

## 6. Worries
- Learning a wrong shape.
- Being slow in front of the family.
- Headset fatigue after a 10-hour shift; a dim living room.
- Setup eating 4 of her 10 minutes.
- Privacy: say "no camera" in the app.

## Top 5 changes
1. A personal word list (client names, medications, places), practised first.
2. A Deaf-reviewed starter pack: about 20 care signs plus 1–10, or a clear handoff to a course.
3. A reading mode with varied, realistic hands, including an older signer's style.
4. One-tap launch, so a full session including setup takes 5 minutes or less.
5. A visible "Deaf-reviewed" credit and a "not an interpreter" notice.

## Review checklist for the built app
1. From the Quest home screen to the first exercise takes 30 seconds or less.
2. She matches her first letter within 2 minutes, without reading instructions.
3. Within 5 minutes she spells M-A-Y-A and gets a verdict for each letter.
4. She types her client's name and one medication and practises both in the same session.
5. When a shape is wrong, the app names the finger in plain words ("bend your index") and she fixes it in 3 tries or fewer.
6. A deliberately wrong M, N or T gets "can't grade", never a pass.
7. Tracking works in a dim living room and with the left hand.
8. Reading mode: she scores 8/10 at the slowest speed, with replay, and the speed goes up only after she succeeds.
9. The next day it opens on her weakest letters.
10. Ten seated minutes cause no nausea and no arm ache, and the text stays readable.
11. Who reviewed the handshapes, and the "not for medical use" notice, are easy to find.
12. After a week she can read her client's name spelled at a natural pace.
